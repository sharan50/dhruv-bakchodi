01 · Overview & investment thesis

An IP company that happens to sell hardware.

Zen Technologies designs combat training simulators and counter-drone systems, owns 100% of the intellectual property behind them, and outsources almost all physical manufacturing. The physical bill of materials is only a quarter to a third of product cost — the rest is software. That single structural fact explains the ~73% gross margins, the insulation from input prices, and why FY26's 29% revenue decline did not dent unit economics.

The thesis in five lines

  • The asset is IP, not plant. 155+ patents filed, 75–85 granted, ₹106.55 Cr of self-funded R&D over five years to FY25 — with no DRDO co-ownership strings.
  • The annuity is real, but half the size consensus assumes. Fitting an installed-base cohort model to eight years of disclosed revenue gives a 10-year economic life and a 7.8% annual attach rate — a lifetime recurring stream worth ~63% of the original sale value, not the 120% carried by the sell-side. See §07b.
  • Regulation is a moat. Buy Indian-IDDM procurement rules bar foreign primes from domestic land-simulator and C-UAS tenders.
  • The binding constraint is order intake, not competitiveness. Gross margin held flat while revenue fell — but ₹44.6 Cr of Q1 inflow against a ₹2,500 Cr year-end target is the number that matters.
  • The price assumes flawless execution. 82–86× trailing earnings, with working capital at 543 days and seven acquisitions still being integrated by a company of under 400 people.
The central tension

Zen is simultaneously the highest-quality business model in Indian listed defence — asset-light, IP-owning, annuity-backed — and a company whose revenue fell 29%, whose order intake has collapsed to a trickle, and whose working capital cycle has nearly doubled. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is the entire investment question.

02 · Business model

Three pillars

Zen's economics are unusual for a defence manufacturer and closer to an industrial software licensor. Understanding the three pillars explains almost every other number in this report.

1 · In-house, self-funded R&D

Most Indian defence peers develop products with DRDO funding and share the resulting rights. Zen chooses its own products, finances its own research, carries the full risk of failure, and retains 100% ownership of its IP. That is the source of both its margin structure and its export optionality.

2 · Asset-light outsourced manufacturing

Fabrication of hardware, metal enclosures, mechanical mounts, vehicle mockups and trailers is completely outsourced to a network of 400+ regional engineering vendors, primarily around Hyderabad. Zen keeps in-house only what carries the IP: final system integration, software installation, precision sensor calibration and quality control.

Where the value sits in a Zen product

Physical bill of materials versus proprietary software and algorithms, share of final product cost

Because the BOM is only 25–35% of final product cost, Zen is largely insulated from raw material price moves and enjoys substantial operating leverage on the way up — and, as FY26 showed, operating deleverage on the way down.

3 · The AMC annuity

Equipment sales carry a warranty — Zen's own support page says 12 months, brokers say 36, and the empirical lag is three years — after which customers transition to Annual Maintenance Contracts. These are signed as discrete five-year blocks, not open-ended service agreements. Management scales the book deliberately to cover fixed operating overheads, protecting base profitability through lumpy procurement cycles.

The widely-quoted figures — 10% attach, 15-year life, 120% lifetime value — originate in broker notes, appear in no Zen filing, and do not survive contact with the disclosed revenue series. Fitted to eight years of actual data, the parameters are a 10-year life and a 7.8% attach rate, giving ~63% of original equipment value over the platform's life. The full derivation, backtest and forward projection are in §07b.

Why exports are structured offshore

Zen operates Zen Technologies USA, Inc. and Zen Defence Technologies L.L.C. (UAE). Three forces drive this: simulators and C-UAS fall under India's SCOMET export-control list requiring slow multi-agency licensing; Indian procurement rules give domestic armed forces orders legal priority over export orders, which delays foreign buyers when manufacturing is India-tied; and roughly 80% of defence product value is captured at design stage rather than assembly. This is a more complete explanation for weak export conversion than any commentary on demand.

What the portfolio assembles — the strategic read

  • A complete unmanned-warfare stack. Propulsion (Vector) → platform and munition (TISA) → ground autonomy (Bhairav) → the weapon station that mounts it (AiTuring) → the electronic warfare that defeats the other side's drones (Unistring plus the organic C-UAS line) → the simulators that train every operator in the chain. No other Indian company, listed or private, holds all of these layers under one roof.
  • Multi-domain simulation. Land (organic) plus sea (ARI, Anawave) plus air (C295 flight simulator) — the precondition for bidding tri-service training programmes rather than Army-only tenders.
  • A second, non-defence leg. ARI's commercial marine, ports and mining business plus DG Shipping e-governance is the group's only MoD-independent revenue — and its cleanest export wedge.
  • The integration showcase. The Integrated Smart Border Suite is what the portfolio looks like fully assembled: sensors, counter-UAS, weapon stations and command-and-control sold as one border-security programme rather than as parts.
The craft, and its cost

Majority-but-not-full stakes keep founders motivated and prices nominal — six capability deals cost less than ₹50 Cr combined. But the costs are already visible: minority interests complicate consolidation, three subsidiaries lost money in FY25, the fire and the related-party advances sit at Unistring, and AiTuring has already been deconsolidated. Cheap options are cheap until you must exercise or abandon them. FY27–28 is when the portfolio has to show which tuck-ins were assets and which were distractions.

How to read FY26

Management's case is that the decline is execution timing: most of the current order book was won from October 2025 onwards with roughly twelve-month execution cycles, so recognition lags. Flat-to-expanding gross and EBITDA margins support that reading — a competitive problem would show up in price. 07b · Deep dive

Product-line economics: the annuity is real, but it is not what the sell-side says it is

Zen reports as a single segment — "Defence and Homeland" — and states explicitly in every filing that segmental reporting under Ind AS 108 is therefore not presented. There is no published simulator-versus-counter-drone revenue split anywhere, in any year. What is disclosed, under the Ind AS 115 disaggregation note, is the split between equipment sales and services. That is the only rigorous product-line cut available, and it turns out to be the more interesting one.

The anomaly

Indexed to FY19, equipment revenue reached 1,335 by FY25. Recurring revenue reached 179. AMC revenue has been effectively flat at ₹33–40 Cr for four consecutive years while the equipment business it is supposed to be a function of expanded thirteen-fold. Any model in which AMC compounds with the installed base is contradicted by the actual numbers. Something has to explain that.

An installed-base cohort model, fitted to the observed series

The mechanic is a lag. Equipment sold in year t earns nothing recurring until warranty expires, then pays an annual fee until the platform is retired. Formally, recurring revenue in year Y is the attach rate r applied to the original value of all kit inside its AMC window:

Model

AMC(Y)  =  r  ×  Σ Equipment(t)   for  t ∈ [Y−L, Y−w]

where w is the warranty lag, L the economic life, and r the annual attach rate as a share of original equipment value. Fitting r in closed form for each (L, w) pair and grid-searching L ∈ [6,20], w ∈ {1,2,3} against observed FY23–FY26 AMC revenue gives a unique minimum.

The 120% figure is wrong, and it is wrong in a specific way

The claim that AMCs return ~120% of original equipment value over a platform's life is repeated in HDFC Securities' and ICICI Securities' notes and has propagated widely. It does not appear in any Zen filing, presentation, annual report or earnings transcript. It is a broker construct, and it is circular: 120% is precisely the identity (15 − 3) × 10%, which is to say it is the other three assumptions multiplied together rather than an independent observation. It is also internally inconsistent in ICICI's own note, which states a ten-year lifespan alongside it — on their own arithmetic that gives 70%, not 120%.

The annuity is real and it is valuable. It is worth roughly 63% of the equipment sale, not 120% — a little over half what the consensus carries. On a ₹1,000 Cr equipment cohort that is a ₹370 Cr difference in lifetime revenue.

Independent corroboration

The fitted attach rate can be checked against a completely separate dataset — the balance sheet rather than the P&L. Zen's AMC order book stood at ₹318.43 Cr at 30 June 2026, and its two publicly disclosed AMC awards (₹55 Cr in Apr 2022, ₹46 Cr in Oct 2024) are both explicitly five-year blocks. That implies a contracted run-rate of ₹63.7 Cr/yr, which against the FY27 eligible base of ₹929 Cr implies an attach rate of 6.86%. Fitted from revenue history: 7.82%. Two independent derivations, one point apart. The model holds.

The annuity wave is already in the ground

This is the finding that matters. The FY24–FY26 equipment cohorts — ₹407 Cr, ₹934 Cr, ₹648 Cr — begin paying AMC in FY27, FY28 and FY29 respectively. The eligible base goes from ₹565 Cr today to ₹2,443 Cr by FY29. Recurring revenue follows it mechanically, with no new equipment orders whatsoever:

Management's stated purpose for the AMC book is explicit — from the Q4 FY25 deck, "scale the AMC business, where the contributions from AMC can take care of the fixed operating expenses." Against an FY26 operating cost base of ₹355 Cr, recurring revenue currently covers 11% of it. On the fitted model it covers 54% by FY29 without a single new order. That is the difference between a lumpy tender business and a business with a floor.

Where this breaks — and it is not a small caveat

The projection above is a potential annuity, not a contracted one. AMCs are signed as discrete five-year blocks with a government counterparty and re-tendered at expiry — a ten-year life therefore requires two sequential renewal events, not one continuous contract. Zen has never disclosed a renewal rate, churn figure or attrition assumption in any filing, transcript, presentation or rating rationale. At 30 June 2026 only ₹318 Cr of the modelled stream is actually under contract. Everything beyond FY28 in the chart above depends on renewals that no public data supports or refutes.

The second exposure is mix. The FY24–FY26 cohorts are heavily counter-drone rather than simulator — anti-drone was ₹504 Cr of the 31 Mar 2025 order book against ₹188 Cr of training simulators. Counter-UAS is a faster-obsolescing category in an active electronic-warfare contest; a ten-year economic life calibrated on tank and small-arms simulators may not survive contact with drone-defeat hardware, where the threat re-specifies every eighteen months. If effective life on that cohort is six years rather than ten, the FY29 figure falls by roughly a third.

What this changes about the investment case

Three things. First, the ₹44.6 Cr Q1 intake print is less catastrophic than it reads — a mechanically rising annuity from already-installed kit lands across exactly the period the intake collapse would otherwise hollow out. Second, the consensus annuity value is roughly half what the sell-side carries, so a DCF built on the 120% figure is materially overstating terminal value. Third, and most usefully, this is the one part of Zen's revenue that is predictable from public data — which makes AMC realisation against the modelled path the cleanest available test of whether management's execution-timing explanation for FY26 is true.

10 · Risks & red flags

The unsoftened version

Ordered by how much each could damage the equity, not by how likely it is.

Order intake has collapsed Critical

Government prioritisation of emergency procurement after Operation Sindoor delayed regular tenders through FY26, producing muted revenue and a subsequent ~50% stock correction. Q1 FY27 fresh inflow was ₹44.62 Cr against a ₹2,500 Cr year-end backlog target. Management has acknowledged there were no recent tenders for either anti-drone systems or simulators. Execution capacity is not the bottleneck; demand conversion is.

Extreme customer concentration Critical

The Government of India and MoD control the bulk of revenue. 63.77% of FY25 consolidated revenue came from just three customers, up from 61.51% in FY24 — concentration is increasing, not diversifying. A single procurement policy shift, budget reallocation or programme deferral moves the entire P&L.

Working capital has nearly doubled Serious

Working capital days went from 283 to 543 as of March 2026, attributed to delayed government validation of complex smart border systems. Receivables climbed to ₹410 Cr in FY25. For a business with 73% gross margins this is not a pricing problem — it is cash conversion, and it means reported profit is increasingly not cash.

Seven acquisitions, under 400 employees Serious

Unistring, AiTuring, ARIPL, ARI Labs, Vector Technics, Bhairav Robotics and Anawave create coordination bottlenecks in an organisation that does not have the headcount to absorb them. Concrete evidence rather than theory: a UTS warehouse fire, and loss-making subsidiaries in FY25 — Zen USA −₹434.71 lakh, Vector Technics −₹14.59 lakh, Zen Medical −₹2.22 lakh. Several holdings are majority-but-not-wholly-owned, which complicates control and consolidation alike.

Valuation leaves no room Serious

82–86× trailing earnings on a company whose revenue just fell 29%, whose ROCE fell from 46% to 16% in two years, and whose order intake is running at roughly 2% of the annual target rate. The multiple is defensible only on the assumption that FY26 was a timing artefact and FY27–28 delivers ₹4,000 Cr. Any slippage is repriced violently — as the 50% drawdown already demonstrated.

Competition is intensifying from both directions Warning

DRDO is actively transferring anti-drone technology to multiple competing players, structurally eroding the technical lead Zen built from 2018. Meanwhile agile startups — Grene Robotics, Big Bang Boom Solutions — are winning counter-drone tenders. Counter-UAS is also the lower-margin part of the mix: gross margin fell from ~75% in FY22 to 60.5% in FY25 partly on that shift.

Governance items to keep in view Warning

The promoter group sold 20 lakh shares in December 2023 for ₹145 Cr. Unsecured related-party advances to UTS creditors stood at ₹5.15 Cr in FY25. On the other side of the ledger, promoter pledging is zero and the board added an independent director with capital-markets and strategic-investment expertise in August 2026. None of this is disqualifying; all of it belongs on a monitoring list.

The export story is aspiration, not backlog Warning

Exports were ~38% of FY25 turnover but are 6.9% of the current order book. The US ambition — $100M within three years against a ~$10bn addressable market — depends on ITAR clearances and prime-vendor partnerships rather than direct bidding, in a market management itself describes as very closed and very difficult. Structural constraints (SCOMET licensing; domestic orders holding legal priority over exports) compound it.

What would change the picture

Two things, in order. First, order inflow reverting to ₹500 Cr+ per quarter — that alone resolves the guidance gap and most of the valuation question. Second, working capital days retracing toward 300, which would confirm the 543-day figure was a government-validation timing artefact rather than a structural change in how Zen gets paid. Watch the 10 August investor meet and the 29 August AGM for both.

12 · Strategic direction

Where the evidence says this is going

Each argument below is built in PEEL form — Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link — so the claim, the facts behind it, the reasoning, and the forward implication stay separable. Everything in the Evidence rows is reported fact drawn from the sections above. The Points, Explanations and Links are inference, and should be read as such.

PEEL 1

From simulator vendor to autonomous-battlefield mini-prime

P
Point

Zen is deliberately repositioning from a training-simulator specialist into an integrated defence-electronics prime for unmanned warfare — selling programmes, not products.

E
Evidence
  • Six acquisitions in eighteen months (Mar 2024 – Nov 2025) assemble a complete unmanned-warfare stack: propulsion, platforms and munitions, ground autonomy, AI weapon stations, electronic warfare, naval simulation.
  • The 2026 product cadence is integration-led: the AI-powered anti-drone system with interceptor and weapon-platform integration (8 May), the Integrated Smart Border Suite fusing multi-agency sensors for Army, BSF and state police (27 May), the high-altitude man-portable system (26 Jul).
  • Investor materials now lead with five capabilities, not simulators. The largest recent order is a system, not a simulator — ₹289 Cr for anti-drone upgrades (Nov 2025) against ₹108–178 Cr simulator awards.
  • The CTO hired in May 2026 is a former DRDO Director General — a systems-programme appointment, not a product one.
  • Management's own long-term guidance is 35% EBITDA / 25% PAT — below FY26's 48.4% / 28%.
E
Explanation

Training alone is a bounded market in which Zen already holds ~80–90% of the Army's installed base — growth there is capped at the budget's growth. Integrated programmes are an order of magnitude larger per contract, and Zen is the only Indian company holding the trainer, the sensor, the effector and the munition in one group. But integration adds hardware revenue at lower gross margin — which is exactly why management guides margins down. The margin guidance is the strategy, stated in numbers: they are telling you the future mix before it arrives.

L
Link

Expect Zen to bid as prime on programme-scale tenders — border security, integrated counter-UAS networks — through FY27–28. The ₹2,500 Cr backlog target implicitly assumes at least one programme-scale win, and the P&L will progressively look more like a defence-electronics company than a software house.

PEEL 2

The ₹1,217 crore war chest is loaded, not parked

P
Point

The balance sheet is positioned for at least one more major acquisition and for the working-capital burden of prime-scale contracts. The cash is a weapon, not a comfort blanket.

E
Evidence
  • ₹1,217 Cr net cash, debt-free (D/E 0.01). The August 2024 QIP raised ₹979.5 Cr net at ₹1,601/share, subscribed 5× by Kotak MF, Motilal Oswal and White Oak.
  • Total disclosed M&A spend since: roughly ₹180 Cr. QIP monitoring-agency reports were still being filed in August 2026 — the proceeds are not fully deployed.
  • Management told the Q1 FY27 call it is actively evaluating "very interesting companies" with "huge revenue potential".
  • Working capital days doubled to 543; receivables reached ₹410 Cr in FY25.
  • Atluri's stated policy: hold enough to survive "two to three years even if there is zero business" — perhaps ₹300–400 Cr at the current cost base.
E
Explanation

Institutional capital was raised for deployment, and the ₹800 Cr gap between what was raised and what has been spent is intent with a balance sheet behind it. Simultaneously, the pivot to prime contracts is cash-hungry: government validation cycles on complex systems — the stated cause of the 543 days — mean Zen finances the state's procurement pipeline from its own balance sheet. The cash is doing two jobs at once, and both are consequences of the same strategy.

L
Link

The evidence points to another ARI-scale (₹100–300 Cr) acquisition within 12–18 months, most plausibly filling the remaining stack gaps — seekers and optics, secure communications, or an export-market beachhead. If instead cash keeps compounding while intake stays weak, expect institutional pressure for buybacks or dividends by FY28.

PEEL 3

Policy is converging on the company Zen already is

P
Point

The political and regulatory environment is moving decisively in Zen's favour — and the FY26 order drought was most plausibly a policy artefact, not a demand collapse.

E
Evidence
  • The FY2026-27 defence budget — the first after Operation Sindoor — rose 15% to ₹7.85 lakh Cr, with capital outlay up 21.8%.
  • On 3 July 2026 the Defence Acquisition Council cleared ~₹52,000 Cr of Acceptances of Necessity, including AKASH TARANG, an anti-UAV electronic-warfare system for the Army.
  • Draft DAP 2026 (February 2026) shifts procurement weight from where a thing is made to who owns the design rights, source code and upgrade authority, formalises spiral development, and makes exportability a baseline qualitative requirement.
  • Buy Indian-IDDM rules already bar foreign primes from Zen's domestic tenders; the ETAI trustworthy-AI framework (October 2024) creates an assurance regime with DRDO as implementer.
  • Post-Sindoor emergency procurement diverted regular tenders through FY26 — management's stated cause of the intake collapse. Zen's launch dates (Sindoor anniversary, Kargil Vijay Diwas) and the high-altitude system aimed at mountain-frontier requirements show a company courting the political moment deliberately.
E
Explanation

Zen is the rare listed company whose business model predates the policy that now rewards it: 100% owned IP, self-funded R&D, source code in-house, manufacturing outsourced. Emergency procurement is by nature a bulk purchase of proven, ready systems; regular tendering — where Zen's IDDM advantages actually bind — is what resumes afterwards. The ₹52,000 Cr of AoNs is the leading indicator of that resumption, because AoNs precede RFPs by quarters, not years. One nuance cuts both ways: a fresh conflict flare-up would raise long-term budgets again while freezing regular tenders again — the same mechanism that produced FY26.

L
Link

If the policy-artefact reading is right, tender flow visibly resumes between Q2 FY27 and Q1 FY28, with counter-UAS programmes playing directly to the May–July 2026 launches. The test is dated: material order announcements by the Q3 FY27 results in January 2027. Silence through that window would mean the drought is structural — DRDO transferring anti-drone technology to competitors, startups taking share — and this thesis needs rewriting. Also watch DAP 2026's finalisation: the draft is favourable; the final text is the fact.

PEEL 4

The export second act is real intent, half-built machinery

P
Point

The export ambition — a rebuilt 30%+ mix, $100M from the US inside three years — is genuine strategy backed by structural moves, but the machinery is only half-built and the stated timetable is unlikely to be met.

E
Evidence
  • Exports were ~38% of FY25 turnover; they are 6.9% (₹85 Cr) of the current order book.
  • Zen has placed IP and commercial rights into Zen Technologies USA and Zen Defence Technologies LLC (UAE) — explicitly to route around SCOMET licensing and the legal priority Indian military orders hold over export commitments.
  • The US plan is ITAR clearance plus prime-vendor partnerships, not direct bidding — a market management itself calls "very closed and very difficult". Zen USA lost ₹4.35 Cr in FY25: cost arriving before revenue.
  • Vector reports ~half its propulsion enquiries from overseas; ARI brought DNV Class A certification and commercial marine customers.
  • On 4 August 2026 the board appointed an independent director whose stated brief includes "international investments and technology-focused initiatives".
E
Explanation

The offshore-IP structure solves the two Indian constraints — licensing speed and domestic priority — but creates the American one: ITAR-compliant supply chains, facility clearances and a prime willing to carry an Indian-origin subsystem, none of which is announced yet. ARI's commercial marine line is the softer wedge, because DNV-certified simulation sells internationally without defence gatekeeping. That is precisely why ARI received 72% of the M&A budget: it is the export strategy wearing a naval-simulation label.

L
Link

Expect export progress to appear first in ARI's commercial pipeline and Vector's propulsion sales, with a named US teaming agreement as the catalyst to watch for in FY27–28. Treat $100M-in-three-years as a direction, not a forecast — the structural work says the intent is serious even where the timeline is not.

PEEL 5

The two-year test, dated and falsifiable

P
Point

Everything above converges on one dated test: convert the policy pipeline into roughly ₹2,000 Cr of fresh orders by March 2027 while digesting seven acquisitions — and management has already pre-announced the margin cost of passing it.

E
Evidence
  • Guidance: ₹2,500 Cr unexecuted book by end-FY27, from ₹1,239 Cr in June — net of execution, that implies ₹2,000 Cr+ of inflow in nine months, against ₹44.6 Cr in Q1.
  • ₹4,000 Cr cumulative revenue guided across FY27+FY28; long-term margins guided to 35% EBITDA / 25% PAT versus 48.4% / ~28% in FY26.
  • The load-bearing facts underneath: working capital at 543 days, three loss-making subsidiaries, ROCE down from 46% to 16% in two years, a CFO in post since January and a CTO since May.
E
Explanation

The guidance arithmetic only closes with programme-scale wins of ₹300 Cr and above — which are exactly the contracts that lengthen validation cycles and stretch working capital further. Passing the revenue test makes the cash-conversion test harder; the two constraints are coupled, not independent. Integration is the silent third constraint: a sub-400-person company running eight entities has no spare management bandwidth for a stumble, which is what the wholesale leadership refresh is trying to buy.

L
Link

Bull: policy timing is right and one programme lands — backlog clears ₹2,500 Cr by March 2027, revenue re-accelerates in FY28, and the 82× multiple survives. Base: partial recovery to ₹1,800–2,200 Cr of backlog, guidance quietly rebased, the multiple compresses toward the sector. Bear: intake stays below ₹100 Cr a quarter through FY27 and working capital holds above 500 days — a second guidance miss on an 80× multiple produces a severe derating, and the ₹1,217 Cr of cash becomes the floor under the equity rather than the fuel for the thesis.

The company the evidence describes in FY29

If the two-year test is passed even partially: a defence-electronics group of nine to ten entities doing ₹1,800–2,200 Cr of revenue at margins nearer 35/25 than FY26's 48/28 — counter-UAS and integrated systems out-earning simulators, naval and commercial marine as the second leg, munitions as a small consumable annuity, and exports rebuilding through the US/UAE structure with ARI's DNV-certified commercial products as the wedge.

The identity shift is the point: from "the simulator company" to an autonomous-battlefield mini-prime — and the risk profile shifts with it, from software vendor (high margin, low capital) to programme prime (concentration, working capital, timing). Sooner or later the multiple will be asked to decide which of those two companies it is pricing.

The §07b product-line model

The recurring-revenue deep dive is original analysis, not extraction, and rests on a separate primary-source pass. Historical revenue FY13–FY22 was rebuilt from Zen's own audited quarterly filings and annual reports (20th through 30th). The equipment/services disaggregation comes from the Ind AS 115 note as reproduced in ICICI Securities' July 2024 initiation (FY19–FY22), disclosed AMC percentages (FY23–FY24), and the quarterly standalone highlights slide (FY25–FY26).   • The 36-month warranty, 10% attach and 120% lifetime figures are broker claims, not company disclosure. None appears in any Zen filing. Zen's own support page states a 12-month warranty; Atluri's only lifecycle statement on the record is ten years.

  • FY13–FY17 equipment/service shares are estimated at 19–21% services from the two years where annual reports disclose it. The fitted life of 10 years is unchanged across every assumption from 10% to 40%, so this does not drive the result.
